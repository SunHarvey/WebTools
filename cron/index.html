    Read and check a cron schedule

    A cron expression is a compact schedule made from minute, hour, day-of-month, month, and day-of-week fields. This parser explains numeric five-field expressions and calculates upcoming wall-clock times with your browser’s IANA timezone data.

    How to use the cron parser

    1. Enter five fields in order.Separate minute, hour, day of month, month, and day of week with spaces.
    2. Choose the schedule timezone.Use an IANA zone name such as UTC or America/New_York. The preview follows that zone’s calendar and daylight-saving transitions.
    3. Parse and inspect the preview.Review each field and the next five matching times. The calculation uses the current browser time when you press the button.

    Common cron examples

    Every five minutes

    */5 * * * * selects every fifth minute, throughout every hour and day.

    Weekdays at 09:00

    0 9 * * 1-5 runs at 9:00 on Monday through Friday in the selected timezone.

    First day of each month

    30 8 1 * * runs at 08:30 on calendar day 1.

    Supported syntax and timezone behavior

    This page accepts exactly five numeric fields with wildcards, comma-separated values, ascending ranges, and positive steps, for example 1,15, 9-17, */10, and 1-20/2. A slash step must follow a wildcard or range; a bare number such as 5/2 is not accepted. Minute is 0–59, hour 0–23, day of month 1–31, month 1–12, and day of week 0–7 (Sunday may be 0 or 7). When neither day field begins with *, a date qualifies if either field matches. If either field begins with *—including a stepped wildcard such as */2—both day fields must match. This follows common Vixie/Cronie-style day-field behavior.

    Month and weekday names, seconds, macros such as @daily, and scheduler-specific extensions such as L, W, and # are not supported. Different cron implementations have dialect-specific details; verify the expression against the scheduler that will run it.

    For this preview, nonexistent wall-clock times inside a spring-forward gap are omitted, and a time repeated during a fall-back overlap produces both matching instants. Cron daemons may catch up skipped jobs or suppress duplicate runs according to their own policies, so this is a civil-time schedule preview—not a simulation of a specific daemon. Timezone rules come from the browser runtime, and the search is limited to six years ahead. Input and results remain in page memory only; this tool does not make network requests or store the expression.

    Cron expression FAQ

    What are the five cron fields?

    They are minute, hour, day of month, month, and day of week, in that order. This tool does not accept a seconds field.

    How do day of month and day of week work together?

    If neither day field begins with *, either field may match. If either begins with *, both must match; for example, */2 is not the same day-field marker as a bare *.

    Does this support every scheduler’s cron dialect?

    No. It supports a documented numeric five-field subset. Names, macros, and scheduler-specific extensions are rejected; check the target scheduler’s documentation before deploying a schedule.

    Is my cron expression uploaded?

    No. Parsing and timezone calculations run in your browser. The expression is not submitted to a server, sent to an external API, or written to browser storage.
